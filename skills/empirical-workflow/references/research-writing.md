# Research Writing

Apply these rules to papers, research memos, reviews, response letters, slide
content, code comments, and other durable research artifacts. Conversation
formatting belongs to the active runtime and is not governed here.

## Claims and effects

- Every substantive claim needs a citation, a registered result, an observed
  fact, or an argument stated in the artifact. Delete an unsupported assertion
  or label it as the author's judgment with its rationale.
- An effect statement gives its direction, magnitude, and a benchmark in units
  the audience understands: currency, a baseline share, a standard deviation,
  or another justified scale. Statistical significance does not describe
  practical importance.
- Keep uncertainty that changes interpretation. Use direct statements for
  settled points and calibrated language for imprecise or conditional results.
- Follow an abstract method statement with a concrete instance. A claim of
  breadth names the covered domains and an example from each.

## Limitations and deviations

- Put a limitation where the relevant design, measurement, model, or result is
  introduced. A later limitations section may consolidate those constraints
  but may not be their first appearance.
- Use first person for an authorial choice. Name its price in statistical
  power, identification, generalizability, construct validity, or model
  dependence.
- Separate data limitations from method limitations. Record the affected claim
  and propagate the restriction to the title, abstract, introduction,
  discussion, and conclusion.
- Disclose every deviation from a preregistration or earlier plan at the point
  it occurs. Include its reason, timing relative to outcome inspection, and
  decision-log reference.

## Sources, summaries, and quotations

- Report a source in indirect speech and in original wording. Text retained
  verbatim is quoted and carries a page, section, table, figure, theorem, or
  other stable locator.
- State whether the version read was the version of record, a preprint, a
  working paper, or an abstract. Do not describe an abstract as if full text
  was read.
- Preserve source punctuation inside a quotation. Never invent a DOI, page,
  quotation, bibliographic field, or citation key.
- Write the final deliverable once after its evidence and structure are
  settled. Scratch reasoning is not copied into the artifact as filler.

## Direct prose

- Prefer prose for connected arguments and lists for genuinely enumerable
  items. Avoid bold labels scattered through paragraphs, title-case headings,
  decorative emphasis, and emoji in research artifacts.
- Avoid negative parallelism such as "not just X, but Y". State the positive
  proposition directly.
- Do not manufacture a three-part slogan. Preserve a genuine enumeration that
  happens to contain three items.
- Remove trailing editorial clauses that announce what the sentence is doing.
  Give a separate sentence to a real claim.
- Prefer full stops to semicolons. Avoid em dashes in authored prose; preserve
  them in quotations and source data.
- Start a paragraph for a new idea. Use one claim per sentence unless one long
  sentence is carrying one continuous argument.
- Prefer plain verbs and stable terminology. Repeat the technical noun instead
  of cycling through near-synonyms. Unpack noun stacks with verbs and
  prepositions.
- Remove conversational scaffolding and assurance language from durable work.
  Begin with the finding, decision, problem, or requested content.
- Avoid mannered metaphors when a literal description is available.

## Editing restraint

Leave sound prose alone. Do not flag a formal term, a long sentence carrying
one argument, a genuine three-item enumeration, source punctuation, or a
technical expression merely because it is stylistically marked. When editing
an author's prose, report what changed and why and provide a diff when the
runtime supports it.

Common model-generated editorial words to remove unless they carry a precise
technical meaning include: delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, showcase,
pivotal, crucial, seamless, meticulous, boasts, notably, importantly,
effectively, carefully, particularly, and crucially. "Significantly" is
reserved for statistical significance.
